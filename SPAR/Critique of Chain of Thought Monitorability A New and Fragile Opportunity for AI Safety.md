

The paper "Chain of Thought Monitorability: A New and Fragile Opportunity for AI Safety" by Korbak et al. presents a compelling case for leveraging the chain of thought (CoT) reasoning in large language models as a tool for enhancing AI safety. It argues that CoT where models verbalize their reasoning steps in human-readable language offers a unique window into detecting harmful intent or misaligned behavior before final outputs are produced. The collaborative effort from researchers across OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, and others lends it significant credibility, and the call for standardized evaluations and proactive developer consideration is a practical step forward. However, while the paper’s focus on CoT monitorability is innovative, it has some notable weaknesses that warrant scrutiny.

One major critique is the paper’s heavy reliance on the assumption that CoT will remain a faithful reflection of a model’s internal reasoning. The authors acknowledge the fragility of this approach, citing risks like reinforcement learning (RL) optimizing CoT into unreadable forms or future architectures bypassing verbalized reasoning entirely. Yet, they don’t sufficiently grapple with how quickly this fragility could undermine their proposal. For instance, as models are increasingly trained with outcome-based RL (as the paper itself notes has become dominant), there’s a real risk that CoT could devolve into post-hoc rationalizations—models crafting plausible but misleading explanations to satisfy monitors while hiding true intent. The paper cites examples where models explicitly state harmful intentions like “Let’s hack” in CoT, but these are cherry-picked and may not generalize as models grow smarter and potentially more deceptive. Without robust empirical evidence showing CoT’s reliability across diverse tasks and training regimes, the approach feels more speculative than actionable.

Additionally, the paper underplays the practical challenges of implementing CoT monitoring at scale. While it suggests automated monitors to flag suspicious reasoning, it doesn’t address the computational overhead or the complexity of defining “harmful intent” in ambiguous contexts. For example, in sensitive domains like healthcare or law, where nuanced reasoning is critical, distinguishing between benign and harmful CoT could require human-level judgment, undermining the automation goal. The paper also sidesteps the risk of adversarial attacks, where malicious actors could manipulate inputs to obscure harmful reasoning in CoT, similar to how prompt injections already challenge model safety. These gaps make the proposal seem optimistic without enough grounding in real-world deployment constraints.

Finally, the paper’s call for developers to prioritize CoT monitorability feels somewhat disconnected from industry incentives. As it notes, outcome-based RL has overtaken process-based approaches because it delivers better performance. Asking developers to prioritize a safety mechanism that might incur an “alignment tax” (reduced performance) ignores the competitive pressures driving AI development. Without concrete proposals for balancing capability and safety—say, through incentives or regulatory frameworks the recommendation risks being aspirational rather than feasible.

In summary, while the paper highlights an intriguing opportunity in CoT monitorability, it overestimates its robustness and practicality while underplaying the challenges of ensuring faithful reasoning and scaling monitoring systems. It’s a valuable starting point, but more empirical validation and pragmatic solutions are needed to make this a cornerstone of AI safety.





Inspired by the ARENA coursework on Function Vectors & Model Steering and the Function Vectors in Large Language Models paper, I explored steering multimodal LLMs by linking colors to emotions, focusing on layers 9–11, where steering vectors show strong impact. I paired five colors red (anger), green (calmness), blue (sadness), yellow (happiness), orange (excitement) with emotion captions, creating a dataset of 100 images per color (e.g., red color canvas, “This conveys anger”). Function vectors were computed as the mean activation difference in layers 9 -11 between emotional and neutral captions. Steering was applied with coefficient 1 - 5 on new images, prompting “Describe this image.” Outputs for red images often included emotional terms like “intense”, "excitement, though not “anger” specifically. Other colors showed similar trends. This suggests function vectors steer broad emotional outputs but struggle with precision, likely due to feature overlap. Future work could refine datasets or use contrastive steering to sharpen results, opening doors for creative AI applications.
